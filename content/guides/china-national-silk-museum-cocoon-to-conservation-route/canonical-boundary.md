# Canonical boundary

Reviewed: 2026-09-04

## Owner statement

This page owns one task: helping a first-time visitor follow a process-and-evidence route through the China National Silk Museum (中国丝绸博物馆) in Hangzhou. The route connects sericulture and raw fibre, reeling and thread preparation, loom and weave structure, labelled historical textiles, Silk Road evidence, and textile conservation. It answers what to see, what each kind of display can establish, and how to recover if a hall is unavailable.

The primary intent is **execute**: choose a route and read the museum without collapsing living craft, old objects, replicas, trade interpretation and conservation into one vague idea of “ancient silk.” The canonical owner is the museum-wide cocoon-to-conservation decision route, not a list of every gallery or temporary exhibition.

## Current-search evidence and gap

Google results were checked on 2026-09-04 for English and Chinese combinations of `China National Silk Museum what to see`, `worth it`, `route`, `reservation passport`, `silk making museum Hangzhou` and `中国丝绸博物馆 怎么逛`. The visible results were dominated by commercial attraction summaries, list pages and user reviews. They generally listed galleries or praised the collection but did not give an object-led route or explain what a model loom, excavated textile, provenance label and conservation process can and cannot prove.

Visible People Also Ask questions were broad—such as which Chinese city is famous for silk, whether silk was more valuable than gold, and how long China kept silk “secret.” Those questions indicate curiosity but do not define this page. A Google-generated overview also repeated an advance-reservation claim that conflicts with the museum’s current official notice for individual visitors. Search snippets and generated summaries are therefore demand evidence only, never operational authority.

Recent traveler discussions show three practical frictions: confusing the museum with a silk-shopping stop, not knowing which halls deserve limited attention, and wanting to understand “real silk” through a museum visit. They are used only to identify questions. They do not prove current access, product authenticity, opening hours or exhibit status.

## Included

- A Homeground editorial route through the Sericulture Hall, Weaving Hall, Silk Road Hall and Textile Conservation Gallery, conditional on same-day access.
- The production chain needed to read displays: mulberry and silkworm care, cocoon, reeling, thread preparation/dyeing, weaving and embroidery.
- A compact distinction among fibre/material, weave structure, technique, motif and finished object.
- A label-reading method: object name, material, structure/technique, date, findspot or provenance, collection route and original/replica/model status.
- Evidence boundaries for Silk Road interpretation: movement and exchange can be supported without reconstructing one unbroken route or an unnamed trader.
- Conservation as documentation, analysis, stabilisation, support and controlled display—not cosmetic renewal.
- The difference between UNESCO recognition of living knowledge and a museum object’s age or authenticity.
- Current admission/passport guidance, unresolved photography and step-free-access evidence, and hall-closure recovery.
- Decisions for first-timers, families, textile specialists, fashion-focused visitors and people who need a shorter or mobility-aware route.

## Excluded and handed off

- A general history of Chinese silk, every dynasty or every Silk Road branch.
- A Hangzhou itinerary, hotel area, airport/station transfer or West Lake route.
- Buying silk, testing fibre at a shop, product pricing, bargaining, factory outlets or authenticity guarantees.
- Booking a workshop, claiming completion of the full craft chain in a short visitor session, or promising a live demonstration.
- A catalogue of every temporary exhibition, event, guided tour, school programme or fashion show.
- A Suzhou silk factory, Jingxian Xuan-paper workshop, Zhoucheng tie-dye workshop or other craft destination.
- A second generic guide to reading archaeological museums or heritage authenticity.
- An assertion that every object in a national museum is ancient, excavated, on display or made entirely of silk.

## Editorial judgments

- Starting with sericulture and weaving before the historical galleries is a Homeground route recommendation, not a mandatory museum route.
- Ending with conservation is an editorial choice because it makes the limits of surviving textile evidence visible.
- The four-question label test and traveler-specific route matrix are Homeground interpretation built from official exhibition scope and conservation sources.
- A named temporary exhibition is not required to complete the route.
