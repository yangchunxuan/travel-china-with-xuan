## Direct-answer hypothesis for the first 150 words

Choose Yueyang Tower for historic timber fabric and conservation work; choose Yellow Crane Tower to read Tang-poetry memory, a changed Wuhan viewpoint and an explicit 1980s reconstruction together; choose Tengwang Pavilion to see how Wang Bo's text helped a vanished building be designed again in the modern period. The three are not three original towers preserved for a millennium. The current Yellow Crane Tower was completed on a new site in 1985; Yueyang Tower's current main Qing form dates to the 1880 rebuilding, while its 1983–1984 dismantling conservation retained, repaired and replaced different components; the current Tengwang Pavilion, a reinforced-concrete structure informed by a twentieth-century design sketch and Song architectural language, was completed in 1989. What survives is different at each: material, location and view, literary fame, and later design imagination.

This is a drafting hypothesis, not body copy.

## Narrative spine

All three current silhouettes communicate “old,” but they preserve different things. Start by separating first-found/first-built dates from the fabric in front of the traveller. At Yellow Crane Tower, follow a lost Qing tower, loss of the old footprint to bridge works and a larger new-site concrete reconstruction. At Yueyang, follow an 1880 timber building, its limited relocation and a conservation dismantling that retained and replaced different components. At Tengwang, follow a text more continuous than the building and a modern designer working from a concept sketch rather than an ancient construction drawing. End with a preservation-mode choice, not a beauty ranking.

A non-fiction opening can juxtapose five eaves and the Yangtze bridge, Yueyang's timber helmet roof on a city-wall platform, and Tengwang's high base and wings beside the Gan River. Do not invent weather, voices, a personal climb or a visitor opinion.

### Required site-specific observation set

Yellow Crane Tower:

1. Compare the present five-eave silhouette with a rights-cleared Qing-period image; do not call it a replica without qualification.
2. Find a reconstruction date/site explanation and record the 1985/current-site distinction.
3. If it is currently displayed, keep the surviving Qing copper roof/top remnant (`铜顶`) separate from the modern main building; do not promise a sighting before current confirmation.
4. Use the visible Yangtze bridge to explain why the old and present viewpoints are not the same.
5. Classify the “Li Bai put down his brush” story as folklore/literary anecdote, not biography.

Yueyang Tower:

1. Read helmet roof, three-storey proportion, columns and jointed timber system from the side before reading the famous text.
2. Distinguish the first-floor replica calligraphy screen from the second-floor Qing screen.
3. Look upward for joints, beams and ceiling work rather than photographing only plaques.
4. Use the lake-facing side to understand the viewing function without claiming a Three Kingdoms view.
5. If currently displayed, read the “Five-Period Tower Views” models as modern interpretations of changing forms, not as Five Dynasties artefacts.

Tengwang Pavilion:

1. Read high base, main pavilion and side wings as one modern composition from the Gan River side.
2. If a model or timeline is currently displayed, use it to establish the destruction/rebuilding line before entering themed rooms.
3. Read faux-timber brackets while remembering the structural core is reinforced concrete.
4. Classify murals, sculptures, digital experiences and actor recitations as modern curation.
5. Compare the present urban river view with literary imagination without saying it is Wang Bo's exact view.
