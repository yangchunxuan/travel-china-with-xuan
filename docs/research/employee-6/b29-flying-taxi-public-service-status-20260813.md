# B29 research-only status — Can tourists ride a flying taxi anywhere in China?

- Candidate: **B29**
- Chinese working title: **游客可以在中国哪些地方乘坐飞行出租车**
- Research status: **RESEARCH ONLY — DO NOT CREATE A GUIDE OR PUBLIC URL**
- Review date: **2026-08-13 (Asia/Shanghai)**
- Canonical question: Is there a stable, publicly ticketed passenger eVTOL service in China that an independent traveller—especially a foreign visitor—can book with clear eligibility, insurance and recovery terms?
- Direct answer on the review date: **No service passed all five publication gates.** Guangzhou and Hefei have certified EH216-S aircraft and certified operators, but the latest first-party filing and July 2026 field reporting still describe internal/invited or commercial trial operations, not an open public ticket channel. Other cities show plans, demonstrations, infrastructure or aircraft still in certification. Homeground should not publish a “where to ride” guide yet.

## The five gates

A place enters a public traveller guide only if all five are evidenced for the same operator, aircraft, location and current period:

1. **Commercial operating authority:** a named operator holds the relevant CAAC operator certificate, and public evidence identifies the approved operation rather than only the manufacturer's aircraft certificates.
2. **Public ticket sale:** a consumer can see dated availability, total price, terms and a payment path—not merely register interest, request a business consultation, reserve a product, attend an invited demonstration or visit an exhibition.
3. **Foreign-visitor eligibility:** accepted identity documents, nationality/residency restrictions, age/weight/health rules and any Chinese-mobile-number or app dependency are stated. Silence is not acceptance and is not exclusion.
4. **Insurance and liability disclosure:** the operator identifies the insurance types and amounts it must disclose to consumers, plus accident/contact and claims handling. A general legal duty to insure is not the same as traveller-facing policy terms.
5. **Safety and disruption workflow:** the exact aircraft is airworthy for the approved use, the operation has weather/technical cancellation rules, passenger briefing and emergency procedures, and the customer has a refund or rebooking path. Certification is a gate, not a promise of zero risk.

## What the certificates do—and do not—prove

The four commonly mentioned certificates answer different questions:

- **TC (Type Certificate):** the approved type design meets the certification basis. CAAC's EH216-S special conditions describe a two-occupant unmanned aircraft system and the safety basis developed for its design and intended use.
- **PC (Production Certificate):** the manufacturer has an approved production quality system for the certified design. CAAC reported issuing EH216-S the first production certificate for a passenger-carrying unmanned aircraft system in April 2024.
- **AC (Standard Airworthiness Certificate):** an individual aircraft conforms to the approved design and is airworthy at issuance/inspection. It is aircraft-specific.
- **OC (Air Operator Certificate):** a named operator meets CCAR-92 requirements for the operations and limitations approved in its operation specifications. CAAC's standard form explicitly says the detailed limitations sit in the approved operation specifications and uses a QR code for verification.

Even all four together do **not** prove that tickets are on sale, a particular date has inventory, a foreign passport is accepted, insurance amounts have been disclosed, or the service will fly in the weather. Those are consumer-operation facts that must be checked separately.

## Public-sale audit

The most important timeline is not the certification headline but the ticket state:

1. **30 March 2025:** EHang announced the two operators' OCs and said citizens and consumers would be able to buy tickets at related Guangzhou and Hefei operation sites. This was an authorization/commercialization announcement, not a captured public checkout.
2. **Q3 2025:** EHang's investor presentation described the EHang Trip ticket system as an **internal beta** allowing employees to book, in preparation for future public online ticketing.
3. **12 March 2026 presentation:** EHang targeted public ticketed sightseeing in Guangzhou and Hefei for March 2026.
4. **8 April 2026 Huangpu government report:** the Guangzhou project was still described as launching “soon,” with online booking to come.
5. **9 June 2026 Q1 filing:** EHang said it and the operators were satisfying additional CAAC operational and safety requirements **ahead of launch**, while routine internal trial commercial operations continued.
6. **14–15 July 2026 independent field check:** *National Business Daily* reported that both sites had not formally opened sales; the Hefei site confirmed no public ticket channel.
7. **24 July 2026 expo reporting:** China News Service still called EH216-S's state “commercial trial operation.”

The later, harder evidence controls. A past intention to sell tickets cannot override a later filing and site check showing that launch remained pending.

## Foreign-visitor eligibility gap

No public terms located in this audit answer the minimum foreign-traveller questions:

- Is a foreign passport accepted as the passenger identity document?
- Is a mainland Chinese phone number or real-name account required?
- Can a non-resident complete payment and identity verification?
- Are there nationality, residence, language, age, body-mass, mobility, pregnancy or medical limits?
- Can a companion translate the safety briefing, or is a specified language required?
- Does the product treat the booked person as a “passenger” or an invited test/experience participant under a separate consent process?

Editorial rule: mark all six **unknown**, not “foreigners allowed” and not “foreigners banned.” There is no public consumer product from which to infer either conclusion.

## Insurance and claims gap

CCAR-92 section 92.667 requires an operator entering a consumer contract to provide full, truthful information about its operating qualifications, service standards, insurance types and insured amounts. Section 92.669 requires liability insurance. China's January 2026 low-altitude-insurance policy also says the compulsory-liability-insurance framework and model clauses are still being built out, with an initial system targeted for 2027.

For the two closest candidates, this audit found no public passenger contract showing:

- insurer and policy type;
- passenger injury/accident cover versus third-party liability;
- insured amount, exclusions and territorial/operational scope;
- whether a waiver affects remedies;
- incident notification and claims contacts;
- refund handling when a flight is cancelled before departure.

Do not replace these missing consumer disclosures with a sentence such as “the flight is insured.” A statutory duty, an operator's private policy and the protection a particular passenger can claim are different facts.

## Safety evidence and editorial limits

Confirmed evidence:

- CAAC created a special certification basis for EH216-S, issued type and production approvals and regulates unmanned-aircraft operations under CCAR-92.
- The two named operators received OCs according to EHang's primary filing.
- EHang's 9 June 2026 filing says the operators were improving procedures, ground support, training and emergency response while meeting additional requirements before public ticketing.

Issuer-reported evidence:

- EHang reported more than 3,000 safe missions, zero accidents and zero violations across the two certified operators since their OCs. Retain the attribution; do not convert it into an independently audited rate or a prediction.

Still missing for travellers:

- site-specific approved operation limitations;
- weather and technical dispatch limits in public terms;
- passenger briefing, accessibility and evacuation arrangements;
- baggage and prohibited-item rules;
- public incident reporting and claims channel;
- cancellation, refund and rebooking conditions;
- a sustained sample of ordinary paying passengers rather than employees, invitees or demonstration riders.

No aviation operation is made “safe” by editorial promise. Homeground should explain evidence and uncertainty, never guarantee an outcome.

## Audit gaps and non-inferences

- The CAAC administrative-results portal and OC QR workflow were not sufficient in this audit to reproduce each operator's complete current operation specifications. Do not infer unlimited geography or passenger categories.
- No live public ticket checkout was located. Absence in search does not prove that no invitation-only or closed-group ride occurred; such rides fail the public-service gate anyway.
- No foreign-passport rule was located. Do not state “foreigners accepted” or “Chinese citizens only.”
- No public insurance schedule was located. Do not state “uninsured”; state that traveller-facing type/amount/claims terms were not verified.
- A manufacturer's cumulative flight count combines contexts and countries and is not a route-level accident rate.
- A passenger-carrying demonstration, trial operation, exhibition, vehicle preorder, training camp, helicopter ride or powered-glider experience is not evidence of a public flying-taxi product.
- “Air taxi” should eventually be reserved for public passenger transport. A short sightseeing loop must be labelled sightseeing, not point-to-point urban transport.
